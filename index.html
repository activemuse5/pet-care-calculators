Guides

The thinking behind the calculators, with the same worked examples you can check above.

How old is my dog in human years? Both methods explained

Almost everyone has heard the rule: multiply your dog's age by seven. It is simple, memorable — and wrong. Dogs do not age at a steady seven-times speed. They mature very fast in their first two years, then slow down, and larger dogs age faster in later life than smaller ones. A single multiplier cannot capture any of that.

Worked example — a 5-year-old medium dog The “×7 rule” says 35 human years. The veterinary chart says 39: year 1 counts as 15, year 2 adds 9 (24 total), and the next three years add 5 each for a medium dog — 24 + 15 = 39. The UCSD epigenetic formula says 57: 16 × ln(5) + 31 ≈ 56.8, which rounds to 57.

Why do the two real methods disagree? They measure different things. The veterinary chart (published by the AVMA) is a practical life-stage guide built from how dogs develop and age in the clinic — when they mature, and when size-related ageing kicks in. The UCSD formula (Wang et al., Cell Systems, 2020) compares DNA methylation patterns — chemical marks on DNA that change predictably with age — between Labrador retrievers and humans. It captures the rapid early-life ageing the chart also shows, but runs “older” in mid-life because methylation ageing and life-stage ageing are not the same clock.

Which should you use? For everyday decisions — when to switch to a senior diet, when to start twice-yearly vet checks — the chart is the practical tool, and it is the one that adjusts for size. The epigenetic number is a fascinating comparison and a good reminder that the first year is worth far more than seven. Either way, both are population averages: your individual dog's health matters more than any converted number.

How much should I feed my dog? The RER method vets use

The feeding guide printed on a bag of dog food is written for an average dog that may not resemble yours — it cannot know whether your dog is neutered, elderly, or training for agility. Vets start from a different place: your dog's resting energy requirement, or RER — the calories needed at complete rest, calculated from body weight — then scale it by a life-stage factor to get the daily target (MER).

Worked example — a 20-lb neutered adult dog 20 lb is 9.07 kg. RER = 70 × 9.070.75 = 366 kcal/day. A neutered adult's factor is 1.6, so MER = 366 × 1.6 = 585 kcal/day. With a food at 350 kcal per cup: 585 ÷ 350 = 1.67 cups per day — split across meals, and not counting treats.

The factor does the heavy lifting. An intact adult uses ×1.8; an inactive or weight-prone dog ×1.2; a puppy under four months ×3.0, dropping to ×2.0 after that. Picking the wrong factor changes the answer far more than rounding ever will — a 20-lb puppy needs nearly twice the food of a 20-lb inactive adult.

Then reality-check it. These formulas estimate a starting point, not a verdict. Individual needs vary — commonly by 20% or more either way. Feed the calculated amount, then check body condition every 2–4 weeks: you should be able to feel ribs under a slight fat covering, and see a waist from above and the side. If your dog is gaining, trim the amount by about 10%; if losing, add about 10% — and raise any persistent change with your vet rather than this page.

Dog pregnancy timeline: the 63 days

A dog's pregnancy is short — about 63 days from mating, with a normal range of roughly 58 to 68 days. Because ovulation timing varies, count the due date as the middle of a window and plan around the whole of it. This is an overview to help you plan vet visits; it is not a substitute for them, and it deliberately does not attempt to diagnose anything.

  • Weeks 1–2: Fertilisation and implantation. Nothing is visible from outside; keep food, exercise and routine normal.
  • Weeks 3–4: Embryos develop. Some dogs eat a little less briefly. This is the usual time for a first vet visit to confirm the pregnancy and discuss care.
  • Week 5: Puppies are recognisably forming. Appetite usually returns and grows; your vet may suggest adjusting food gradually and can discuss an ultrasound or later X-ray to estimate litter size.
  • Weeks 6–7: The abdomen enlarges and weight gain speeds up. Set up a quiet whelping area so she can get used to it, and confirm your vet's out-of-hours contact details.
  • Weeks 8–9: Final preparation. Milk may appear, and many breeders monitor temperature as whelping approaches — ask your vet what to watch for in your dog's case and exactly when to call.

When to see the vet: book in as soon as you suspect pregnancy, again mid-pregnancy as advised, and call promptly if the 68-day mark passes with no labour, if labour stalls, or if anything worries you at any point. Only your vet, who can examine your dog, can judge whether things are progressing normally.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers; the guides above carry the detail.

Is the “1 dog year = 7 human years” rule true?

No. Dogs mature much faster than that rule suggests in their first two years — the first year of a medium-sized dog's life is roughly equal to 15 human years, and the second adds about 9 more. After that, each dog year adds roughly 4 to 7 human years depending on size, with larger dogs ageing faster. That is why a 5-year-old medium dog works out to about 39 human years on the veterinary chart, not 35.

How many cups of food does a 20-lb dog need?

Using the RER method vets use: a 20-lb dog (9.07 kg) has a resting energy requirement of about 366 kcal per day. A typical neutered adult needs about 1.6 times that, roughly 585 kcal per day. With a food containing 350 kcal per cup, that is about 1.67 cups per day. Your dog's real need varies with activity and body condition, so adjust in small steps and check body condition every 2–4 weeks.

How long are dogs pregnant?

About 63 days from mating on average. The normal range is roughly 58 to 68 days, so a due date is best treated as the middle of a window rather than an exact day. If a pregnancy runs past 68 days, or you are unsure of the mating date, contact your vet.

Can these calculators replace a vet?

No. These calculators give general estimates from published formulas — they cannot examine your pet, and they do not diagnose, treat or dose anything. Always confirm feeding plans, growth concerns and pregnancy care with your own veterinarian, who knows your animal.
